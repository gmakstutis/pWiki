# Constructive Alignment: From Curriculum Design to Institutional Practice

> Last updated: 2026-08-13

## Overview
This synthesis traces constructive alignment (CA) as it moves from a unit-level curriculum design principle to an institution-wide policy framework. It draws together Biggs's two formulations of the concept (2003 and 2014), the empirical evidence on its effects, and the critique that emerges from Deeley et al.'s (2019) study of student dissatisfaction with assessment and feedback. The central thread is that CA is not merely a technical alignment of outcomes, teaching, and assessment, but a claim about how students learn and a framework for institutional quality that carries unresolved tensions.

## Key Claims

### Claim 1: CA rests on a constructivist account of learning
The 'constructive' aspect of CA holds that students construct meaning through relevant learning activities; meaning is not transmitted from teacher to learner but created by the learner. Biggs grounds this in the psychology of constructivism and traces it back to Tyler's (1949) four questions about educational purposes, experiences, organisation, and evaluation, and to Shuell's (1986) dictum that what the student does matters more than what the teacher does.
Source support:
- [Biggs Constructive Alignment](../source-notes/biggs-constructive-alignment.md), p. 1
- [Biggs 2014 Constructive Alignment in University Teaching](../source-notes/biggs-2014-constructive-alignment-university-teaching.md), pp. 6-8

### Claim 2: The ILO verb is the mechanism of alignment
Alignment is achieved when the same verb appears in the intended learning outcome, the teaching/learning activities, and the assessment tasks. The verb in the ILO becomes the common link that establishes alignment across the three components. High-level verbs (reflect, hypothesise, solve unseen problems) demand demonstration of understanding, whereas low-level verbs (describe, identify, memorise) permit surface learning.
Source support:
- [Biggs 2014 Constructive Alignment in University Teaching](../source-notes/biggs-2014-constructive-alignment-university-teaching.md), p. 8
- [Biggs Constructive Alignment](../source-notes/biggs-constructive-alignment.md), p. 2

### Claim 3: Assessment is the lever that drives student behaviour
Because students learn what they think they will be assessed on, assessment is the curriculum from the student's perspective. Faulty assessment practices do more damage by misaligning teaching than any other single factor. This is why CA insists that assessment tasks must mirror the ILOs, and why it goes beyond criterion-referenced assessment by aligning teaching methods as well as assessment tasks.
Source support:
- [Biggs Constructive Alignment](../source-notes/biggs-constructive-alignment.md), p. 3
- [Biggs 2014 Constructive Alignment in University Teaching](../source-notes/biggs-2014-constructive-alignment-university-teaching.md), p. 5

### Claim 4: CA is empirically associated with deep learning
Constructively aligned teaching appears to produce high quality learning outcomes and student satisfaction. In high-CA units, students were significantly more likely to adopt deep approaches and less likely to use surface approaches than students in low-CA units (Wang et al., 2013).
Source support:
- [Biggs 2014 Constructive Alignment in University Teaching](../source-notes/biggs-2014-constructive-alignment-university-teaching.md), p. 13

### Claim 5: CA's institutionalisation is both its success and its problem
The most important development since CA was first published is its incorporation into institutional teaching policy. But implementation institution-wide faces real obstacles: finance and staff workloads, and teacher resistance to change. Biggs frames the shift from quality assurance (reactive) to quality enhancement (proactive) as the way forward, subsuming QA under QE.
Source support:
- [Biggs 2014 Constructive Alignment in University Teaching](../source-notes/biggs-2014-constructive-alignment-university-teaching.md), pp. 14-19

### Claim 6: Student dissatisfaction reveals the limits of alignment
Deeley et al. (2019) show that student dissatisfaction with assessment and feedback is a 'wicked' problem with no single elegant solution. Misalignment between staff and student expectations is a key source of dissatisfaction, and linking assessment and feedback explicitly to ILOs (i.e., CA) can help. But the problem is systemic, not individual: feedback often has little impact despite the effort put into producing it, and students want feedback that is 'timely, detailed and actionable' delivered within an 'ethos of care'.
Source support:
- [Deeley et al 2019](../source-notes/deeley-2019-wicked-problem-assessment-feedback.md), pp. 386-399

## Tensions and Unresolved Questions
- **Alignment vs. wickedness.** Biggs presents CA as a coherent, optimisable system; Deeley et al. present assessment and feedback as a 'wicked' problem with no single solution. Can a well-aligned system still be a wicked problem, or does the wickedness arise precisely where alignment breaks down?
- **Specification vs. emergence.** CA requires ILOs to be specified in advance, but some disciplines (and some learning) resist advance specification. How far can the verb-based mechanism stretch before it distorts the learning it is meant to enable?
- **Enhancement vs. assurance.** Biggs's move from QA to QE is normative and aspirational. Whether institutions actually make this shift, and whether QE can be sustained under financial and workload constraints, remains open.
- **Student voice.** Deeley et al. foreground the relational and emotional dimension of feedback ('ethos of care'), which the technical language of alignment tends to background. Whether CA can accommodate this relational dimension is unresolved.
