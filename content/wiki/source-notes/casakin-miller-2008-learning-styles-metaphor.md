# Individual Learning Styles and Design Performance in the Metaphorical Reasoning Process

**Author:** Hernan P. Casakin & Kevin Miller
**Year:** 2008
**Source type:** article
**Raw file:** [Casakin & Miller 2008.pdf](../../raw/articles/Casakin%20%26%20Miller%202008.pdf)

## Summary
This theoretical study examines the relationship between Kolb's experiential learning theory (and its four learning styles) and the use of metaphor in design problem solving. The authors describe the component processes of metaphorical reasoning (identification and retrieval, mapping and transference, and adaptation and application), analyse how each of the four learning styles might perform in each phase, and propose a series of hypotheses to be tested in future empirical work. They suggest that assimilators, followed by converging learners, hold learning preferences most advantageous for the successful use of metaphor, while diverging learners and accommodators are the most disadvantaged.

## Key Claims
- Metaphors are not merely decorative — they also structure and organise our thoughts (Ricoeur, 1977) (p. 277).
- Metaphors allow for the transfer of concepts and ideas from remote domains to the problem at hand (p. 277).
- The basic processes of metaphorical reasoning can be organised into three major phases: identification and retrieval, mapping and transference, and adaptation and application (Gentner, 1983) (p. 278).
- Metaphorical reasoning serves for the purposes of stimulating a reflective dialogue with the design situation (Schön, 1983b) (p. 279).
- Kolb's ELT suggests learning to be a dynamic and cyclical process, and considers reflection fundamental to our ability to learn (p. 282).
- Learning styles are not determined by inherited characteristics, but develop through experience (Kolb, 1984) (p. 284).
- The four learning styles are: Diverging (concrete experience and reflective observation), Assimilating (abstract conceptualisation and reflective observation), Converging (abstract conceptualisation and active experimentation), and Accommodating (concrete experience and active experimentation) (p. 285-286).
- No single learning style appears to be consistently strong across all the phases of the metaphorical process — each has both advantages and disadvantages that vary with the different requirements (p. 290).
- Assimilators, followed by converging learners, hold learning preferences most advantageous for the successful use of metaphor (p. 290).
- Diverging learners and accommodators are considered to be the most disadvantaged learners with regard to metaphorical reasoning (p. 290).
- Awareness of the differences of learning styles might provide studio teachers with a refined tool to train students in metaphor use (p. 290).
