# Exploring the 'Wicked' Problem of Student Dissatisfaction with Assessment and Feedback in Higher Education

**Author:** Susan J. Deeley, Moira Fischbacher-Smith, Dimitar Karadzhov & Elina Koristashevskaya
**Year:** 2019
**Source type:** article
**Raw file:** [Deeley et al 2019.pdf](../../raw/articles/Deeley%20et%20al%202019.pdf)

## Summary

This paper explores the meaning, challenges and potential opportunities for enhancement in assessment and feedback within the authors' own institution (the College of Social Sciences at the University of Glasgow), as illustrative of approaches that can be taken elsewhere. Using a qualitative design, a review of assessment and feedback, which included an exploration of students' perceptions, was made in one College of the University. The findings highlighted variations in assessment and feedback practice across the College, with dissatisfaction typically being due to misunderstanding or miscommunication between staff and students. The authors assert that students' dissatisfaction with assessment and feedback is not a 'tame' problem for which a straightforward solution exists, but a 'wicked' problem that requires a complex approach with multiple interventions.

## Key Claims

- Widespread student dissatisfaction with assessment and feedback practices in higher education, as evidenced by the National Student Survey (NSS), presents a complex and multi-faceted, 'wicked' problem (Grint, 2008) (p. 385).
- Being a 'wicked' problem infers that social relationships and interactions are central to the issue and that there is no single elegant solution (p. 386).
- Student dissatisfaction with assessment and feedback is a symptom, rather than the cause, of a problem (p. 386).
- The difference between formative and summative assessment can be a misleading dichotomy (Boud, Cohen, & Sampson, 1999) because summative assessment may also provide rich learning opportunities for students if constructive feedback is provided (p. 386).
- 'For many students, feedback seems to have little or no impact, despite the considerable time and effort put into its production' (Sadler, 2010) (p. 387).
- The understanding and expectations of assessment and feedback can differ between students and staff, and significant differences can emerge between what students want and what staff provide (p. 387).
- Linking assessment and feedback processes explicitly to the aims and intended learning outcomes of courses and programmes, or what Biggs and Tang (2011) refer to as 'constructive alignment', can contribute to students' understanding (p. 388).
- Students described effective feedback as 'timely, detailed and actionable' (Smith et al., 2016) (p. 399).
- Students respond positively to staff who are approachable, and willing to offer help, support and encouragement (p. 399).
- This personal approach to feedback reiterates what Sutton (2012) refers to as an 'ethos of care' which is conducive to enhancing student learning (p. 399).
- Student dissatisfaction is not an individual problem but one that belongs to a system, in other words, a 'wicked' problem for which 'no-one has the solution in isolation' (Grint, 2008) (p. 397).
- Written summative examinations and written summative assignments, such as essays, were the most commonly adopted and heavily weighted assessment methods, accounting for an average of 80.41% of the course grade (p. 392).
- Students perceived assessment to be important for testing knowledge, but also for gaining transferable skills valuable for their future employability (p. 396).
