# HE in FE: Vocationalism, Class and Social Justice

**Author:** James Avis & Kevin Orr
**Year:** 2016
**Source type:** article
**Raw file:** [Avis & Orr 2016.pdf](../../raw/articles/Avis%20&%20Orr%202016.pdf)

## Summary

This paper examines Higher Education in Further Education (HE in FE) in England, drawing on the Wolf (2015) report and HEFCE's Participation of Local Area (POLAR) classifications, alongside key literature. It interrogates conceptualisations of vocationalism and its articulation with class and gender. The paper argues that while HE in FE has limited traction in facilitating social mobility, it does serve as a resource in the struggle for social justice.

## Key Claims

- HE in FE has limited traction in facilitating social mobility but does serve as a resource in the struggle for social justice (p. 2).
- The expansion of HE, in England referred to as Widening Participation (WP), has been seen as pivotal to the development of knowledge-based economies, but the claims made for the knowledge-based economy are overstated (p. 2-3).
- Wolf doubts the sustainability or economic justification of the knowledge economy stance and suggests that the development of vocational education in Further Education would make a greater contribution to skill development, warning against the colonisation of vocational education by universities (p. 3).
- The government defines social mobility as "a measure of how free people are to improve their position in society" — a narrow understanding that suggests a restricted conception of tackling inequality, entailing society's divisions remaining in place while a few enterprising individuals may climb over them (p. 6).
- Lingard, Sellar and Savage refer to this as an equity model, which reduces social mobility to a technical issue that ignores broader philosophical questions concerned with social justice (p. 6).
- Any relative measure of social mobility "necessarily implies downward mobility for children from rich/middle income families" (Crawford et al., 2011) (p. 6).
- The proportion of children on free school meals that went on to enrol at the 25 most selective universities in England between 2005-06 and 2007-08 was just 2 per cent, compared with 72.2 per cent for other state educated students and 25.8 per cent for privately educated students (p. 6).
- Between 2008 and 2012 the number of entrants to part-time HE courses in England fell by 37 per cent; the number of part-time undergraduate students in 2013-14 was half what it was in 2010-11 (p. 7).
- HE in FE students are more likely than university students to come from areas that have had low levels of participation in HE (p. 8).
- There is a steady differential of 40 per cent in the level of participation between the lowest and highest POLAR quintile: in 2011-12, 60 per cent of young people in quintile 5 accessed HE while the figure for quintile 1 was 20 per cent (p. 8-9).
- The proportion of graduates from FE colleges in 2010-11 employed full-time in professional occupations was 8 per cent, compared with 23 per cent for graduates from HEIs (p. 9).
- Mean starting salaries for HE in FE graduates were 16 per cent lower than those from HEIs in 2010-11 (p. 9).
- While HE in FE has widened participation, it has not systematically enabled relative upward social mobility at least as measured by income (p. 9).
- "Education cannot compensate for society" (Bernstein, 1970), but this is not to claim that education cannot be a resource in the struggle for social justice (p. 10).
- The concept of human capital renders individuals responsible for their own perpetual development to enhance their value in the market place, regardless of structural restrictions (p. 11).
- "If you work hard enough you can attain your dream" is "the hope-goading gloss on the other side of sterner neoliberal injunctions" (Zipin et al., 2015) (p. 11).
- Meritocracy "does not aim to create a classless society, but it legitimizes the given hierarchical structure and empowers people to be socially re-classified" (Liu, 2011) (p. 12).
- HE in FE courses can help to enlighten lives and expose opportunities for many individual students, even if they have not consistently led to upward social mobility at a macro level (p. 13).
- "The privileged access of the powerful to theoretical abstract knowledge provides them with the ability to mobilize knowledge to think the unthinkable and the not-yet-thought" (Wheelahan, 2010) (p. 13).
- FE colleges, like their international counterparts such as Community Colleges, can only reflect and not transform unequal societies, despite the rhetoric of human capital (p. 14).
