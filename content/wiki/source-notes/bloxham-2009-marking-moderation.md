# Marking and Moderation in the UK: False Assumptions and Wasted Resources

**Author:** Sue Bloxham
**Year:** 2009
**Source type:** article
**Raw file:** [Bloxham\_2009.pdf](../../raw/articles/Bloxham_2009.pdf)

## Summary

This article challenges a number of assumptions underlying marking of student work in British universities. It argues that, in developing rigorous moderation procedures, we have created a huge burden for markers which adds little to accuracy and reliability but creates additional work for staff, constrains assessment choices, and slows down feedback to students. It explores whether there are other ways to provide confidence in marking and grading that might divert this energy into productive activities with useful outcomes for students and learning.

## Key Claims

- Four assumptions are examined: (1) we can accurately and reliably give a mark to most students' work; (2) internal moderation ensures fair and appropriate standards; (3) external moderation ensures consistent standards across the UK sector; (4) students' final award reflects their achievement consistently (p. 209).
- Higher education assessment is internally set and marked, which means marking is largely in the hands of the subjective judgement of tutors and assumes academics share common views regarding academic standards (p. 210).
- Elton and Johnston (2002) describe marker reliability as low for essays and problem style examinations except where mere knowledge recall is required (p. 210).
- Knight and Yorke (2003) argue that complex learning cannot be reduced to something simple enough to measure reliably; the more complex the learning, the more we draw on 'connoisseurship' (Eisner 1985) rather than measurement to make our judgements (p. 210).
- A positivist approach believes in the importance of validity and reliability, assuming objective standards can be set; an interpretivist approach rejects objective truth and conceives of assessment as based on a local context, carried out through the judgement of experts in the field (p. 210-211).
- There is 'an underlying softness in the data that typically goes unrecognised' (Sadler 2005) (p. 211).
- Experienced assessors come to see themselves as expert markers, but studies have found that experienced markers are no better than novice markers at applying standards consistently (p. 211).
- Moderation is a process for assuring that an assessment outcome is valid, fair and reliable and that marking criteria have been applied consistently (p. 212).
- One key difficulty in sample second marking is the dubious assumption that a sample can be 'taken as indicative of the whole' (Partington 1994) (p. 213).
- The external examiner system is designed to bring a level of external accountability to assessment decisions, but there is limited research evidence on its effectiveness and what exists does not inspire confidence (p. 213).
- Recent reports suggest the UK degree classification system is no longer fit for purpose, with procedures for classifying degrees inconsistent both within and between universities (p. 213-214).
- Although reliable procedures give the appearance of 'good order', they do not necessarily deliver good quality assessment practice (p. 214).
- QA procedures may have a potentially detrimental effect on student learning, with the illusion of confidence skewing assessment design away from that which supports learning towards that which serves mainly 'certification' and 'quality assurance' (p. 214).
- Biggs (2003) suggests that the use of external examiners may discourage innovative assessment practices (p. 215).
- A programme approach assumes that although each assignment's mark may have limited reliability, confidence should come instead from the professional judgement of several different tutors across a large number of different assessment opportunities (p. 216).
- Moderation at the individual assignment level rarely makes changes of the order needed to affect a student's overall profile, so the impact of individual item moderation on a student's overall classification is likely to be very limited (p. 216).
- Involving students as partners in assessment: 'self assessment is a sine qua non for effective learning' (Black and Wiliam 1998) (p. 217).
- At heart this is an epistemological issue: how is the knowledge of what is a good exam answer, essay, project or piece created? It is created through a social process involving dialogue and experience (p. 218).
