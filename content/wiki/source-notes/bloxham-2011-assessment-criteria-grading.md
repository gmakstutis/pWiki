# Mark My Words: The Role of Assessment Criteria in UK Higher Education Grading Practices

**Author:** Sue Bloxham, Peter Boyd & Susan Orr
**Year:** 2011
**Source type:** article
**Raw file:** [Bloxham et al 2011.pdf](../../raw/articles/Bloxham%20et%20al%202011.pdf)

## Summary
This article seeks to illuminate the gap between UK policy and practice in relation to the use of criteria for allocating grades. It critiques criterion-referenced grading from three perspectives (socio-cultural, cognitive, and empirical). Twelve lecturers from two universities were asked to 'think aloud' as they graded two written assignments. The study found that assessors made holistic rather than analytical judgements, that a high proportion of tutors did not make use of written criteria in their marking, and that norm referencing was an important part of the grading process despite published criteria.

## Key Claims
- Assessment in higher education involves decentralised, subject-specific decision-making processes, given credence in the UK by processes of quality assurance involving national agencies, external examining, and local moderation (p. 1).
- The 'production, publication and discussion of clear assessment criteria' is now regarded as 'a sine qua non of an effective assessment strategy' (Woolf 2004) (p. 2).
- The drive for the setting and use of assessment criteria does not have the theoretical or research support that one might have assumed (Sadler 2008) (p. 2).
- Three key sources of criticism of the accountability paradigm: socio-cultural, cognitive, and empirical (p. 2).
- Orr (2007) argues that 'validating practices' for assessment are based on a techno-rationalist approach and a positivist model of assessment standards (p. 3).
- Assessment is 'primarily understood as technology', and this has disguised its role in social reproduction (Delandshere, drawing on Bourdieu) (p. 3).
- The techno-rational view of knowledge equates publication with explicitness, but "Assessment consists in the exercise of an applied skill, and there are core aspects of this knowledge practice that cannot be captured by a mere propositional description of them" (Gonzalez Arnal and Burwood) (p. 3).
- Eisner (1985) refers to the use of 'connoisseurship' — the well-informed subjective judgement which accrues through immersion in a subject discipline (p. 3).
- Higher education assessment is a 'socially situated interpretive act' (Shay 2004) (p. 3).
- The accountability paradigm assumes criterion-referenced assessment is possible without reference to student norms, that we can write unambiguous statements of criteria, that we can allocate different ranks of marks across criteria reliably, and that we can mentally manipulate a complex set of explicit criteria whilst reading student work (p. 3-4).
- Sadler makes a strong case that an analytic approach is theoretically and practically deficient: by limiting itself to preset criteria, it cannot take into account all the necessary nuances of expert judgements (p. 4).
- The study found that tutors came to a holistic conclusion regarding the final mark; they did not show evidence of linear or discrete processing of individual criteria (p. 8).
- Two patterns of marking strategy: an initial engagement with 'cues' leading to 'banding', then a checking/refining stage; or a rapid move through banding to a grade decision (p. 8).
- Markers are not cynically referring to criteria post hoc to defend their judgements, but are using them to help refine 'hunch' decisions (p. 8).
- Four of the 12 tutors did not have any assessment artefacts to hand whilst marking, and two further tutors had artefacts available but did not use them (p. 9).
- Two-thirds of the markers used some form of norm referencing in making their marking decisions (p. 11).
- Written criteria/standards only take on meaning once the staff apply their personal 'standards framework' to them, and other students' work is crucial in forming that framework (p. 12).
- The fluid nature of these frameworks, constructed and reconstructed during the act of marking, further undermines the notion that we can predetermine and publish accurate criteria to students (p. 12).
- Staff place considerable focus on surface characteristics whilst marking, but strive not to make them significant in grading decisions (p. 13).
- The disjunction between stated policies and actual practices is not the result of a cynical rejection of accountability, but a rational response to the contradiction between espoused policies of accountability and the reality of professional judgement (p. 14).
- Marking standards are social constructions and not some fixed entity which staff are more or less able to perceive (p. 14).
