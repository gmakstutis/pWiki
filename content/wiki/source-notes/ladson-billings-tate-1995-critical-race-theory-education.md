# Toward a Critical Race Theory of Education

**Author:** Gloria Ladson-Billings & William F. Tate IV
**Year:** 1995
**Source type:** article
**Raw file:** [Ladson_Billings_1995.pdf](../../raw/articles/Ladson_Billings_1995.pdf)

## Summary
This article asserts that despite the salience of race in U.S. society, as a topic of scholarly inquiry it remains untheorized. It argues for a critical race theoretical perspective in education analogous to that of critical race theory in legal scholarship by developing three propositions: (1) race continues to be significant in the United States; (2) U.S. society is based on property rights rather than human rights; and (3) the intersection of race and property creates an analytical tool for understanding inequity. The article concludes with a look at the limitations of the current multicultural paradigm.

## Key Claims
- Despite the salience of race in U.S. society, as a topic of scholarly inquiry, it remains untheorized (p. 47).
- The discussion of social inequity in general, and school inequity in particular, is based on three central propositions: race continues to be a significant factor in determining inequity; U.S. society is based on property rights; and the intersection of race and property creates an analytic tool through which we can understand social and school inequity (p. 48).
- Race, unlike gender and class, remains untheorized (p. 49).
- Class- and gender-based explanations are not powerful enough to explain all of the difference (or variance) in school experience and performance (p. 51).
- U.S. society is based on property rights rather than human rights (p. 52).
- Racism is not a series of isolated acts, but is endemic in American life, deeply ingrained legally, culturally, and even psychologically (Delgado) (p. 52).
- Critical race theory challenges the 'traditional claims of legal neutrality, objectivity, color-blindness, and meritocracy as camouflages for the self-interest of dominant groups in American society' (Delgado) (p. 52).
- The intersection of race and property creates an analytic tool through which we can understand social and school inequity (p. 58).
- Whiteness — that which Whites alone possess — is valuable and is property (Harris) (p. 59).
- The 'property functions of whiteness' include: rights of disposition; rights to use and enjoyment; reputation and status property; and the absolute right to exclude (p. 59).
- The current multicultural paradigm functions in a manner similar to civil rights law: instead of creating radically new paradigms that ensure justice, multicultural reforms are routinely 'sucked back into the system' (p. 62).
- Critical race theory in education, like its antecedent in legal scholarship, is a radical critique of both the status quo and the purported reforms (p. 62).
