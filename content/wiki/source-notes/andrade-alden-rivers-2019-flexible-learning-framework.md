# Developing a Framework for Sustainable Growth of Flexible Learning Opportunities

**Author:** Maureen Snow Andrade & Bethany Alden-Rivers
**Year:** 2019
**Source type:** article
**Raw file:** [Andrade & Alden-Rivers 2019.pdf](../../raw/articles/Andrade%20&%20Alden-Rivers%202019.pdf)

## Summary
This article presents a theory-based and practice-informed framework for the scalable expansion of flexible learning, encompassing online learning, blended learning, competency-based education, and open educational resources. The framework was implemented at a large, open-admission regional university in the US. The authors argue that success for all students in flexible learning can be achieved through course design and responsive pedagogies, supported by competency frameworks and training for teaching staff. The framework's 11 aspects are grounded in two change models: Kotter and Cohen (2002) and Bolman and Deal (2017).

## Key Claims
- Flexible learning is a critical strategy for increasing access to higher education, involving choice in "how, what, when and where [individuals] learn: the pace, place and mode of delivery" (HEA, 2015) (p. 1).
- First-time entrants, who may lack the cultural capital to be successful, may be more likely to participate in flexible learning (online and blended) than traditional students, and may need the most support (p. 3).
- Success for all students in flexible forms of delivery can be achieved through course design and responsive pedagogies (p. 3).
- Competency frameworks outlining the digital skills and pedagogies needed by instructors must be developed, with initial and ongoing training provided for teaching staff (p. 3).
- Blocking students from online coursework will not improve outcomes nor provide a long-term solution to increasing demands for higher education (p. 2).
- The framework is based on common elements, goals, and concerns across higher education (faculty governance, student success, quality assurance) and could be adopted by other institutions with adaptations (p. 5).
- The 11 framework aspects include: vision, instructional design, institutional expertise, policy and infrastructure, analytics, quality assurance, strategic campaigns, student success, faculty development and recognition, shared governance, and shared understanding (p. 5-9).
- Change is much more likely to stick if it is institutionalized in policy (p. 7).
- Quality assurance processes demonstrate that faculty controls the quality of online courses, reducing a key barrier to change — faculty concerns over quality (p. 8).
- Higher education institutions are "loosely coupled," so leaders must take a collaborative approach involving faculty (p. 10).
